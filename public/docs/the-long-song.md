# \# The Long Song

# 

# \*A foundational story for Stammespillet, built on the world described in the lore bible ("Utviskingen"). Written in English to match the game's production language. Clan names, place names, and the protagonist are invented for this story — the actual student groups will still name their own clans, and nothing here overrides what they create. Think of this as one true telling of how the five clans of the lore bible first learned what they had forgotten, following the five-act structure (the Crack, the Envoy, the Anchors, the Gathering, the Choice) and ending in the Rebuilding. Roughly 10,000 words.\*

# 

# \---

# 

# \## I. The Crack

# 

# Wren was eleven verses into the Long Song when her grandmother stopped her with two fingers against her collarbone — the old signal for \*wrong note, try again\* — and Wren realized she had sung the same line twice without noticing.

# 

# "You're thinking about the delta," said Elder Sula. She did not say it unkindly. She never said anything unkindly; she simply said things, the way water says things to a stone, over years, until the stone changes shape whether it wants to or not.

# 

# "Everyone's thinking about the delta," Wren said.

# 

# That much was true. The Drift — the tangle of lashed-together boathouses that had been the Tideborn's home for longer than anyone could sing — used to float. Now, at low water, half of it sat in the mud like a fish too tired to swim, and the children played a game of walking from hull to hull without ever touching the cracked grey clay beneath, because touching the clay meant admitting the water wasn't coming back.

# 

# "Then think about it in tune," said Sula, and made Wren start the verse again.

# 

# The Long Song was not one song. It was three hundred years of songs sewn together, verse by verse, family by family, and no one living had ever heard the whole of it end to end — because no one living remembered all of it, and the parts that were forgotten did not leave gaps. They left silence shaped exactly like the missing words, so smooth you could sing straight through it your whole life and never feel the edges.

# 

# Wren was fourteen, which was young for an apprentice singer, but she had the kind of memory that worried people. She could hear a verse twice and keep it forever, every syllable, every place where her grandmother's voice cracked with age in a particular spot so that Wren now cracked her own voice there too, out of respect, or habit, or love — she was never sure which.

# 

# She was still holding the last note when Tam came running down the causeway, and she knew before he said a word, because Tam only ran when something was wrong, and the last time he ran like that it was because his little sister had fallen through a rotted plank into the shallows.

# 

# "Your uncle," Tam said, out of breath. "Fenn. He's — Wren, come \*look.\*"

# 

# The lagoon at dusk was the colour of the inside of a shell, pink and grey and going dark at the edges. Uncle Fenn's skiff had gone out an hour before to check the eel traps, same as every evening, same as he'd done for as long as Wren had been alive.

# 

# Now there was a patch on the water that had no colour at all.

# 

# It was not black. Black was still a colour; black still caught the last light and threw a little of it back. This was — nothing. A flat, smooth, perfectly circular absence, roughly the size of a small boat, sitting on the surface of the lagoon like a held breath. No ripples moved through it. No light bent around it. When Wren made herself look directly at it, her eyes slid off, the way they slid off the sun.

# 

# "Where's the skiff," she said, though she already understood, in the cold back part of her mind that understood things before the rest of her was ready to.

# 

# "That's what I'm telling you," Tam said. "There \*was\* a skiff. Five minutes ago. I saw it. Your uncle was in it. I waved at him."

# 

# They stood at the edge of the causeway and watched the colourless patch do nothing at all, which was somehow worse than watching it do something, and after a while — Wren never knew exactly how long, time behaved strangely near it — the patch simply wasn't there anymore either. The water closed over the place where it had been, ordinary water, catching ordinary light, and a moment later a moorhen landed on it as if nothing in the world had ever been wrong with that patch of lagoon.

# 

# Aunt Mira was mending a net on the porch of the houseboat when Wren and Tam came pounding up the walk, and she looked up smiling, the particular smile of a woman who is not yet worried but is about to be.

# 

# "You've both got faces like you swallowed a fish bone," she said. "What is it?"

# 

# "Fenn," Wren said. "Where's Fenn?"

# 

# Mira's smile did not disappear. It simply had nowhere to go. "Fenn who?"

# 

# "\*Your husband,\* Aunt Mira."

# 

# "I'm not married," Mira said, and laughed, a small confused laugh, the laugh of someone humouring a joke they haven't caught up to yet. Then she looked down at the net in her lap, at the third knot from the left, which was tied in the particular pattern that meant a person's name — every Tideborn net carried its maker's mark knotted somewhere in the mesh, and every net mended by a spouse carried theirs too, twined together, so that even a torn net told you who loved whom.

# 

# There were two names knotted into that net. Mira's own mark, and beside it, a second mark, half-finished, in cord that did not match the rest — newer cord, added partway through, as if someone had picked the net back up after setting it down and not quite remembered how far they'd gotten.

# 

# Mira stared at the second knot for a long time.

# 

# "I don't know whose that is," she said, very quietly, and her voice had lost all the humour in it. "I don't know why I would have started that. I don't—"

# 

# She stopped. Her hands, without her seeming to notice, had begun to unpick the second knot's cord, unravelling it, and Wren realized with a lurch that if she let her keep going, in another minute there would be no knot at all, and no question, and no strangeness — only a net with one name in it, which would make perfect and comfortable sense.

# 

# "Don't," Wren said, and caught her aunt's hands still. "Please. Leave it."

# 

# Mira looked at her, frightened now, though she could not have said of what. "Why does it matter, child?"

# 

# "Because I think someone we loved just went out of that net," Wren said, "and if you finish unpicking it, there won't be anywhere left for him at all."

# 

# Elder Sula heard the whole of it that night, sitting very still with her hands folded, the way she sat when a verse of the Long Song was particularly old or particularly sad.

# 

# "You're certain of what you saw," she said, when Wren finished.

# 

# "I'm certain."

# 

# "And Tam saw the boat before it happened."

# 

# "He saw Fenn wave. Two minutes before."

# 

# Sula was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the Drift creaked and settled the way it always did at night, boathouses easing against their moorings, and somewhere further along the walk a baby cried and was hushed.

# 

# "There are old verses," Sula said at last, "about places that stopped being there. Nobody sings them at festivals. They don't scan well, and nobody likes how they end, which is to say they don't end, they just stop, mid-line, the way a person stops talking when someone puts a hand over their mouth." She looked at Wren very directly. "I want you to understand that I believe you. And I want you to understand that if you say this to the full council tomorrow, half of them will decide you imagined it, and the other half will decide you're right, and be too frightened to do anything except tell you to be quiet about it. Which will you prefer?"

# 

# Wren thought about it honestly, which was the only way she knew how to think about anything Sula asked her.

# 

# "I'll tell the council," she said. "Because someone should know, even if they don't do anything. But I'm also going to keep asking questions. On my own. Because I don't think 'be quiet about it' is going to make the lagoon stop losing pieces of itself."

# 

# Sula almost smiled — the ghost of the expression she wore when a student finally sang a hard passage clean.

# 

# "Good," she said. "Then let's make sure you don't do it alone. Take Tam. He runs well, and he doesn't panic, which you may have noticed you do, slightly, in the second half of a bad night."

# 

# "I do not—"

# 

# "You unpicked your own sleeve-cord twice while you were talking just now," Sula said, nodding at Wren's hands, which had indeed been busy the whole time with a loose thread. "Take Tam."

#

# 

# \## II. The Envoy

# 

# The night before they left, Wren couldn't sleep, and found Tam already awake and sitting on the edge of the causeway with his feet in the shallows, doing the thing he always did when he was nervous and didn't want to admit it: skipping stones at a target only he could see.

# 

# "You don't have to come," she said, sitting down beside him.

# 

# "I know." He skipped another stone. It went four hops and sank. "You'd go anyway. And then I'd spend the whole time you were gone imagining worse things happening to you than are probably happening, which is a worse way to spend a fortnight than actually being there to see what's really going on."

# 

# "That's not a compliment to my judgment."

# 

# "It's not meant to be a compliment. It's meant to be true." He finally looked at her, and in the dark his face had none of its usual easy humour in it. "Wren. Your uncle's boat didn't just sink. I saw the water where it had been. It didn't even \*ripple.\* I keep telling myself I imagined that part, and I know I didn't, and I don't want you finding out what it means without someone else standing next to you who also saw it."

# 

# She didn't have an answer for that, so she didn't try to make one. She just leaned her shoulder against his, the way she had since they were small enough to fit two on one net-mending stool, and they sat like that until the tide turned, which on the Drift was as good as a handshake on a promise.

# 

# Elder Sula found them there at first light, already dressed for travel, and did not comment on the wrongness of a fourteen-year-old being sent to speak for the whole council. She only pressed a folded strip of cloth into Wren's hand — a verse, written out in the careful, old-fashioned notation the Tideborn used when a song mattered too much to risk to memory alone.

# 

# "In case you need proof, in a city that only believes what's written down, that we remember things too," Sula said. "And in case you forget, out there, that you're allowed to come home."

# 

# There was a great deal of talk about grief doing strange things to the mind of a young girl who had loved her uncle very much, and a smaller, quieter amount of talk from the elders who remembered other odd silences over the years — a fishing ground that had simply never been productive again after a certain summer, a cousin's name no one could quite place in the family songs though everyone agreed there ought to be one there — talk that trailed off before it went anywhere, because nobody wanted to be the one who said the word that made it real.

# 

# What the council did agree on, in the end, was something almost by accident: they would send an envoy to Vantor, the walled city at the center of all the old roads, to request a fresh copy of the True Map — officially, to settle a dispute about where the delta's boundary now lay, since the water had pulled back so far that half the fishing grounds Vantor taxed no longer existed. Unofficially, three of the elders privately hoped that if there was anything strange happening to the shape of the world, Vantor's archivists — who wrote everything down, who forgot nothing on purpose — might have noticed it too, and might, being the City Clan and therefore insufferably thorough, have already given it a name.

# 

# "Send Wren," said Elder Sula, when the council had argued itself half-exhausted over who should go. "She's the one who saw it. Let her ask the question with her own voice, not secondhand through some elder who'll soften it into nothing by the time it reaches Vantor's ears."

# 

# It was, everyone agreed, unusual to send someone so young as an envoy. Wren suspected — though no one said so directly — that the council was also, quietly, glad to have her go somewhere else for a while, so that whatever strangeness followed her would follow her away from the Drift.

# 

# Tam came without being asked twice.

# 

# The road to Vantor ran along the old riverbank, past the place where the Dead River used to be a live one, before the Deephewn dammed it two generations back to feed their mines. Wren had grown up hating that dam the way you hate a scar you've had since before you can remember getting it — not with any particular heat, just as a fact of your body, a thing that is simply wrong and simply yours.

# 

# They fell in for half a day with a Kestrin trading caravan headed the same direction — six wagons, a dozen mules, and an old man named Corvan who kept the caravan's ledger chained to his belt like it might otherwise run off and start a life of its own.

# 

# "Tideborn," Corvan said, looking them over with the particular assessing look that Kestrin traders gave everyone, the look of a man mentally entering you into a column. "Long way from water."

# 

# "Envoy business," Wren said. "For the council."

# 

# "Mm." Corvan didn't ask what business. Kestrin traders rarely asked directly; they let you decide how much to owe them by how much you volunteered. "Tell you something for free, then, seeing as you're walking my road. Things have gone strange out past the crossing lately. Lost a whole grazing meadow off the western spur last month. Not burned, not flooded. Just — gone. Cartographer had to redraw four miles of good map into blank paper, and half our factors won't even talk about it, like saying the word'll make it worse."

# 

# Wren's chest went tight. "What word?"

# 

# Corvan glanced sideways at her, and for just a moment something moved behind his professional blandness — not fear exactly, but its cousin, caution.

# 

# "Never mind the word," he said. "You'll hear it soon enough on your own, and you'll wish you hadn't. Tell your council the Kestrin have noticed too. That's worth something between clans, that kind of telling, and I expect to be remembered for it." He said this last part the way a man notes a debit in a book — flatly, as a fact rather than a favor, and Wren understood, even then, that the Kestrin did not believe in kindness that didn't get written down somewhere.

# 

# "One more thing," Corvan added, almost as an afterthought, though Wren would later understand that Kestrin traders rarely said anything as an actual afterthought. "There's a debt on our books. Old one. Owed by the caravan, to be paid one way or another before the season turns. Some of my factors have got an idea about how to pay it that I don't much care for. Involves knowing where the Hollowkin keep themselves this year."

# 

# He did not explain further, and Wren did not yet understand enough to ask the right question. She only felt, obscurely, that she had just been handed a stone with no idea yet what door it might unlock.

# 

# Vantor rose out of the plain like a memory of a bigger city than itself — which, Wren realized as they passed through the outer gate, was exactly what it was. Half the walls were new stone patched onto old foundations three times too wide for the buildings that now stood on them, as if the city had shrunk inside its own skin and pulled the skin tight to hide it.

# 

# Whole districts marked on the gate-keeper's toll-map didn't exist anymore — not ruined, the gate-keeper explained when Tam asked, just \*resettled elsewhere within the walls,\* in the careful bureaucratic phrase Vantor used for streets that had quietly stopped being streets. Nobody in the city seemed to find the phrase strange. That, more than anything else, told Wren how long this had been going on, and how practiced this particular clan had become at not looking directly at its own shrinking.

# 

# While Wren went to present the council's formal request, Tam wandered the lower market, and came back an hour later looking shaken in a way that had nothing to do with the errand.

# 

# "Talked to a fruit-seller," he said. "Asked her how long she'd had her stall in that spot. She said her whole life. Then I asked what used to be on the corner across from her, because there's clearly a gap there, foundations and nothing else, grass growing through the stones. She looked right at it and told me there'd never been anything on that corner. Wren, there were \*foundations.\* You don't get foundations from nothing."

# 

# "Did you tell her that?"

# 

# "I started to. She got frightened. Not angry — frightened, like I'd said something dangerous just by noticing." Tam shoved his hands in his pockets. "I think that's worse than Vantor lying to itself on purpose. I think most of the city genuinely doesn't remember there's anything to lie about."

# 

# The Archive occupied what had once, by the scale of its pillars, been something much grander — a temple, maybe, or a hall of some vanished king. Now it was shelves. Endless shelves, corridor after corridor of them, each one tended by an archivist in grey, and the whole building hummed with the particular hush of a place where silence is a kind of work.

# 

# The young archivist who met them at the request desk introduced himself as Idris, and had the harried, sleepless look of someone doing two jobs badly because he refused to do either of them wrong.

# 

# "A copy of the True Map," he repeated, when Wren explained her errand. "For the delta boundary dispute." He said it the way you'd repeat back an order in a tavern, automatically, while thinking about something else entirely.

# 

# "You've had other requests like it," Wren guessed. "Recently."

# 

# Idris looked up properly for the first time. "Why would you say that?"

# 

# "Because you looked like you'd already had this exact conversation today, before I asked it."

# 

# He was quiet a moment, weighing her, the way Corvan had weighed her, though with less practice at hiding it. "Three requests this month," he admitted finally, dropping his voice. "All border disputes. All along different edges of the map. I've had to authorize four partial redraws, and my superior keeps telling me to just — copy the old lines forward and note the discrepancies as surveying error." He glanced over his shoulder, toward the deeper stacks, and something in the glance made Wren's stomach turn cold. "There isn't survey error on four unconnected borders in one season. There's something eating the edges of the map, and I'm being told to write down that it isn't."

# 

# "What's your superior's name?" Tam asked, in the tone of someone gathering evidence.

# 

# "Doesn't matter," Idris said, too quickly. "It's not — it's not one person's fault. It's fear. Nobody wants to be the archivist who admits the Archive doesn't know something. We're the ones who are supposed to \*not forget.\*" He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "So instead we're starting to lie a little, here and there, in the margins, just to keep the shelves looking whole. I don't love it. I don't know how to stop it either."

# 

# Wren thought of the half-unpicked knot in Aunt Mira's net, and the strange mercy of leaving a question unanswered rather than smoothing it into a comfortable lie.

# 

# "Don't finish unpicking it," she said, half to herself.

# 

# "What?"

# 

# "Nothing. Show me the oldest section of the True Map you've got. Not the newest copy — the oldest one you're not supposed to touch."

# 

# Idris hesitated exactly as long as a young man torn between rules and curiosity always hesitates, and then, because curiosity nearly always wins in people who became archivists in the first place, he took them down.

# 

# The oldest fragment of the True Map was not paper. It was a sheet of something like hammered tin, so old the markings had gone soft and rounded, more felt than read. Wren traced the lines with one finger — rivers that no longer ran where rivers now ran, a lake with no modern name, five settlements clustered so close together on the old map that they nearly touched, where now there were five very separate, very distant homes.

# 

# "That's wrong," Idris said, frowning at it. "The scale's off. No settlement's ever been that close to another out there — the delta's sixty miles from the mountain passes."

# 

# "Unless the map isn't wrong," Wren said slowly. "Unless it's just old enough to be from before the sixty miles existed."

# 

# In one corner of the tin sheet, in a script so worn it took all three of them squinting together to be sure of it, was a single label, marking a spot near where the five clustered settlements sat: \*the First Stone.\*

# 

# Wren felt something in her chest that she would later come to recognize as the particular ache of a verse of the Long Song surfacing in a place she had never expected to hear it — because the Long Song had a fragment, half-forgotten, that her own grandmother sang without ever explaining, a line about \*the stone that remembers before the song did, back when the five were one throat singing.\*

# 

# "I need to go to the Deephewn halls," Wren said.

# 

# "That's not remotely on the way home," Tam pointed out.

# 

# "I know."

# 

# Idris looked between them, and something in his exhausted, honest face decided itself. "I'm coming with you," he said. "If this map is wrong — or old in a way that matters — I want to know before my superior makes me forge another line across it and call it survey error."

# \## III. The Anchors

# 

# \### The Deepest Shaft

# 

# The Deephewn halls did not so much begin as swallow the road — one moment Wren, Tam, and Idris were walking between open hillsides, and the next the hills had become walls, carved and vaulted, torchlit even at midday, and every surface bore writing: histories chiselled into the stone floor to ceiling, generations of them, so that walking the main hall was, in a real sense, walking through the inside of a memory that had never once been allowed to soften. Wren found herself slowing without meaning to, the way you slow down in a room full of people talking, even when the voices are three hundred years silent and made of stone.

# 

# They were received first, briefly and coldly, by Elder Korr himself, who listened to Wren's request for an audience about "the True Map and certain irregularities" with the particular patience of a man counting down the seconds until a conversation could politely end.

# 

# "The dam stands," Korr said, before she'd even finished her second sentence, clearly assuming this was, at heart, another Tideborn grievance wearing a diplomat's clothes. "It has stood for two generations and it will stand for two more. I've heard every argument your council has ever sent us, and I promise you this one will not be the argument that moves me."

# 

# "I haven't come about the dam," Wren said.

# 

# "Everyone from the Drift has come about the dam, whatever they say they've come about." But something in her face must have given him pause, because he didn't dismiss her outright — he simply waved a hand, tired rather than unkind, toward the lower galleries. "Talk to the young ones, if you like. They have more patience for outsiders than I do these days, and considerably less sense about which doors ought to stay shut."

# 

# It was, Wren would later realize, the closest thing to permission Korr was ever going to give anyone for what came next, and he gave it without having the faintest idea he was giving it.

# 

# They were met — more accurately, intercepted, once they'd found their own way further down — by a young stonecarver named Bryn, who had the ash-streaked hands of someone who spent more time in the deep galleries than the sunlit ones, and the particular stubborn set to his jaw of someone used to being told no by people older than him.

# 

# "Tideborn," he said, reading Wren's braided hair-cord with the flat, unfriendly recognition of an old grudge. "Come to complain about the dam again?"

# 

# "Come about something older than the dam," Wren said, and showed him the rubbing Idris had taken of the tin map's corner label. "\*The First Stone.\* Does that mean anything to you?"

# 

# Bryn's stubborn expression flickered, just slightly, into something more complicated.

# 

# "It means the one wall in this entire hall that has no writing on it," he said. "Which, in a hall where we carve everything, means everyone agreed three hundred years ago never to touch it. And it means the deepest shaft, which was sealed a hundred years back, and which the elders have never once explained the reason for — except that the inscription explaining it is the only inscription in Deephewn history that's ever been deliberately ground smooth. Not forgotten. \*Erased.\* On purpose. By our own hands." He looked at Wren with something that was almost relief at finally saying it aloud to someone who wasn't already bored of the argument. "I've been trying to get the elders to let me open the shaft again for a year. They keep saying it'll kill us. They won't say how they know that, since none of them remember why either."

# 

# "Take us there," Wren said.

# 

# "Why should I trust three strangers with the one thing my own people won't discuss?"

# 

# "Because," Wren said, "I think whatever's down there and whatever's eating your dam's dry side of the world and whatever swallowed my uncle's boat on the lagoon two hundred miles from here are the same thing wearing three different faces. And I think your First Stone and my Long Song used to be the same telling, split in half, and neither half makes complete sense anymore on its own."

# 

# Bryn studied her for a long moment — the particular long moment of a person deciding whether a stranger's madness matches a shape he's already half-suspected on his own — and then, without another word, he turned and led them down.

# 

# The deepest shaft had been sealed with rubble rather than a proper door, which told its own story: someone in a hurry, someone frightened enough not to bother with masonry. It took the better part of an hour with Bryn's tools to clear enough of a gap to slip through, and when they did, the air on the other side was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

# 

# The First Stone stood at the center of a round chamber, waist-high, rough-hewn, and covered edge to edge in script older than anything else in the halls above — not Deephewn lettering at all, or not quite, but something that looked like an ancestor of it, the way a grandparent's face sits underneath a grandchild's without quite matching.

# 

# Idris, kneeling to examine it with the automatic hunger of a born archivist, went very still.

# 

# "I can read maybe one word in five," he said. "But — Wren, some of this script repeats. Look, this cluster of marks, here, and here, and here again near the bottom."

# 

# Wren looked, and her breath caught, because she knew that repeating shape. Not from any writing. From sound.

# 

# "That's not just script," she said slowly. "That's notation. It's a \*tune.\* It's been carved like writing, but it's meant to be sung." She closed her eyes, and let the shape of the marks translate itself, haltingly, the way the oldest, strangest fragment of the Long Song always had — the one about \*the stone that remembers before the song did\* — and began, very quietly, to hum it.

# 

# The moment the melody left her throat, Bryn made a small strangled sound, because the marks on the stone — impossibly, though none of them would ever quite agree afterward on whether they'd truly seen it or only felt it — seemed to settle into focus, the way a word you've stared at too long suddenly resolves back into meaning.

# 

# It was not a warning. It was not a curse. It was a promise, made by five mouths speaking as one, in a language neither wholly Deephewn nor wholly Tideborn nor anything else that existed anymore on its own:

# 

# \*We were one people, and we borrowed more than the world could spare, and when the reckoning came we did not have the courage to pay it together, so we tore ourselves into five so that no one of us would ever again be tempted to try to pay it, or steal it, alone.\*

# 

# "That's not a warning against opening the shaft," Wren whispered. "That's the reason the shaft was sealed. Someone came down here, remembered too much at once, and it frightened the elders so badly they erased the explanation rather than live with what it meant."

# 

# Bryn sat down hard on the chilled stone floor, all his stubbornness gone out of him at once. "We were never enemies with the Tideborn," he said, very quietly. "We were never enemies with anyone. We're the same mistake, torn into five pieces so we couldn't finish making it twice."

# 

# Wren felt something settle into her chest — heavy, and sad, and also, against all reasonable expectation, something almost like hope. A fragment. The first whole one. Not a clue toward an answer, but a genuine piece of it.

# 

# \### The Ledger's Price

# 

# They left the Deephewn halls three days later with Bryn insisting on coming — "someone from my clan has to see this through, and it isn't going to be one of the elders" — and caught up with Corvan's caravan again at the Crossing, the sprawling waystation where five roads met and none of them, notably, belonged to anyone.

# 

# Corvan was in a foul mood, hunched over his ledger by lamplight, and it took Wren only a moment to understand why: a knot of his own factors stood arguing in low, urgent voices near the mule pens, and the word \*Hollowkin\* came up twice before she'd even finished crossing the yard.

# 

# "They mean to do it," Corvan said flatly, when Wren asked. "Sell the location. There's a merchant out of the eastern hold who'll clear our whole debt for the coordinates of the Hollowkin's winter camp — he trades in rare barks and roots, wants first pick before anyone else finds them. My factors say a debt's a debt and coin doesn't care whose privacy it's built on." He said this the way he said everything, without obvious anger, simply laying out the numbers. "I haven't decided yet whether to stop them."

# 

# "You have to stop them," Wren said.

# 

# "Do I." Corvan looked at her with the flat, appraising stare she now recognized as his default. "Tell me why, in terms that'll matter to a man who's watched three caravans go under from unpaid debt in his lifetime."

# 

# Wren thought of the First Stone's promise, of five mouths that had once spoken as one, and understood, with a small cold jolt, that she had just been handed the shape of the choice the lore of this whole broken world kept circling back to — the same choice, over and over, in every size: take the easy debt, or pay the harder truth.

# 

# "Because the Hollowkin live closest to whatever's eating the edges of the world," she said, "and if that's frightening enough to keep every other clan from ever going near them, then selling their location to a bark merchant isn't clearing a debt. It's spending someone else's safety to pay for your own mistake. And I don't think the world's in a forgiving mood about people doing that anymore." She held his gaze. "I'm going to warn them either way. You can be the caravan who tried to stop it, or the caravan whose name is in the same sentence as the one that sold them out. Your ledger, your choice which line to write."

# 

# Corvan was quiet for a long moment, turning his stylus over in ink-stained fingers.

# 

# "You Tideborn," he said at last, "have an uncomfortable talent for turning a plain business matter into a matter of conscience." But he closed the ledger with a snap, and rose, and went to have a very firm word with his factors, and by morning the plan to sell the Hollowkin's location had, at least for this caravan, quietly died.

# 

# He did not thank Wren for it, exactly. But that evening, before they left, he pressed a small brass token into her hand — a Kestrin debt-mark, the kind traders exchanged when one owed the other something not easily paid in coin.

# 

# "For the telling," he said. "You'll want it kept, not spent. Some day somebody in this world's going to need every clan to remember they're owed a favor by every other clan, all at the same time. I have a feeling you're going to be the one calling those favors in."

# 

# \### The Empty Name

# 

# The Quiet Wood did not so much have a border as a gradual hush — the birdsong thinned, the light went a deeper, dimmer green, and the path, if you could call it a path, kept almost disappearing under moss that seemed to grow specifically to discourage remembering exactly where you'd walked. Bryn, who had spent his whole life underground in straight-cut galleries, found the wood almost unbearable, and said so more than once, quietly, to no one in particular.

# 

# "There's no record here," he muttered, at one point, running a hand along a tree trunk as if hoping to find carved letters under the moss and bark. "How does anyone know anything happened, if nobody writes it down or sings it or even names the path they took to get here?"

# 

# "Maybe that's the point," Idris said, surprising himself as much as anyone. He'd been quiet most of the walk, still turning over what he'd seen in the deepest shaft. "We keep assuming remembering is always the brave thing and forgetting is always the frightened thing. What if that's just what an archivist would think, because forgetting is the one failure our whole profession is built to prevent?"

# 

# Nobody had an answer to that, and they walked the rest of the way in a thoughtful silence that suited the wood far better than talk would have.

# 

# They found the Hollowkin camp — or rather, the Hollowkin found them, the way people who live at the edge of vanishing things learn to find strangers before strangers find them — and were brought, without much ceremony, before an elder woman who introduced herself only as Ash.

# 

# "You came to warn us," Ash said, before Wren had said a single word about it, and there was no surprise in her voice at all. "The Kestrin factor already came through yesterday, alone, ahead of you, and told us himself. Called it settling an old debt of conscience. I didn't understand what he meant. Now I think I do."

# 

# Wren felt an unexpected warmth toward Corvan, wherever he now was on the road, keeping his own quiet accounts.

# 

# That evening the Hollowkin held what Ash called, simply, "the letting go" — no grand ceremony, no fire, no chanting, just a circle of perhaps thirty people sitting in the deepening dark, each holding some small object, and one by one setting it down in the center and walking away from it without looking back.

# 

# "We do this every year," Ash explained quietly, while Bryn watched with the stricken fascination of someone raised to carve every memory into permanent stone. "One thing each. A name, a place, a grief too heavy to carry another season. We choose to lay it down before the world takes it from us by force. It's not because we don't feel our losses. It's because we've learned that a thing held too tightly is the first thing the Blank reaches for, and a thing set down freely, in love, seems to matter less to it. We don't know why. We only know it's kept us alive this close to the edge for longer than any of the other four clans would have lasted."

# 

# Partway through the circle, a woman no older than Wren's own mother — Ash introduced her only as Sorrel — set down a small carved wooden bird, and began, very quietly, to weep, though her face showed no memory at all of why.

# 

# "She grieves a child," Ash said, very low, seeing Wren's confusion. "None of the rest of us remember the child ever being born. Sorrel herself doesn't remember it either, not really — she only knows, in the place under memory where knowing lives, that something is missing that should be there. That is what the Blank does, when it takes a person mid-life rather than a place. It doesn't just erase them. It leaves the shape of their absence behind, in everyone who loved them, like a hole you can feel with your hands in the dark but never quite see."

# 

# Wren thought of Aunt Mira's net, of the half-finished knot she had stopped her from unpicking, and understood, with an ache that went all the way down, that she had done for her aunt exactly what this whole grieving circle refused, on principle, to let themselves do for Sorrel's child: she had held on to the shape of an absence rather than smoothing it away. She did not know, even now, whether that had been mercy or cruelty. Perhaps, she thought, it was both, the way most true things were.

# 

# At the end of the circle, Ash came to Wren directly, and pressed something into her hands that was not an object at all, but a single word, spoken so quietly the others could not hear it.

# 

# "The Empty Name," Ash said. "The first thing our people ever chose to forget, three hundred years gone, back when we still had a different name for ourselves entirely. We taught it to ourselves to forget on purpose — not because it was dangerous to know, but because it hurt too much to carry. It has a shape exactly like a missing person, if you listen for it. I give it to you because you are gathering the pieces of something, and I think this is one of them, and because you have shown this camp more care in one evening than any outsider in a generation."

# 

# Wren carried the name back out of the Quiet Wood like a coal cupped in both hands — careful, warm, and a little afraid of what it might do if she closed her fingers too tightly around it.

#

# 

# \## IV. The Gathering

# 

# It should not have worked. Wren understood that even as she was doing it — that a fourteen-year-old singer's apprentice, however unusually gifted, had no business standing in the middle of the Crossing's central yard sending riders in five directions with a request that had not been made successfully in three hundred years: \*come, all five clans, to one place, at one time, and listen.\*

# 

# But the world had changed faster in the last few months than in the years before them. Borders were failing. Maps were being redrawn faster than archivists could hide the seams. Trade roads were ending in blank paper. The delta was dying in public. Even the Hollowkin, who had survived longest by yielding, said the Blank was pressing closer than it had in a generation. The old habit of silence was no longer keeping pace with the damage.

# 

# They came, in the end, more out of dread than hope. The Deephewn sent Elder Korr, stone-faced and suspicious, with Bryn at his side looking both proud and terrified of his own part in dragging his people here. Korr said almost nothing on arrival, only studied the other four delegations the way he might have studied a wall he suspected of concealing a structural flaw. Vantor sent a delegation headed by Idris's own chief archivist, pale and defensive, aware by now that his forgeries had been noticed and uncertain whether he was walking into an accusation or a rescue; he carried, absurdly, a satchel of blank record-paper, as though he meant to start writing the truth down the instant anyone gave him permission to. The Kestrin sent Corvan, ledger under his arm as always, flanked by two of the very factors who'd wanted to sell the Hollowkin out, chastened now and silent, standing half a step behind him like men waiting to be told their sentence. The Hollowkin sent only Ash, alone, because — she said, without any drama in her voice — "the rest of us find it hard to stay whole for long around this much old anger in one place." And the Tideborn sent Wren, because by now no one on the council would have dreamed of sending anyone else, though Elder Sula had come as far as the Crossing's outer gate before stopping, and had simply said, "This part isn't mine to finish. It's yours," and turned back toward home, trusting her.

# 

# The five delegations arranged themselves around the central well of the Crossing in a rough, wary circle, each cluster of people as far from the others as the space allowed, old grudges radiating off them like heat off stone. The Deephewn would not look at the Tideborn. The Kestrin factors would not look at Ash. The Vantor archivist would not look at anyone at all.

# 

# Wren had planned, on the long ride back, to explain it to them — the First Stone's promise, the Empty Name, the shared debt, the one people torn into five. She had rehearsed the explanation a dozen different ways.

# 

# Standing in front of them now, she understood that none of those explanations would work, because explaining was the City Clan's language, and none of these people, deep down, in the part of themselves that had been arguing for three hundred years, trusted explanations. They trusted what their own clan's memory was built to trust: stone, or ledger-ink, or law, or the deliberate shape of an absence. What they had never once, in three centuries, done together was \*listen\* to all five ways at once.

# 

# So instead of explaining, Wren began to sing.

# 

# She started with the oldest fragment of the Long Song, the one about the stone that remembers before the song did, and let its half-broken melody carry the shape of the First Stone's script beneath it, the way Bryn had shown her it could be read. Then, without pause, she folded in the rhythm of a ledger-tally — the flat, repeating cadence Corvan used when reading old debts aloud, which she had listened to for three days on the road until it had become, almost without her noticing, a kind of music itself. Then she let the Empty Name settle into the melody where a name belonged and had been missing for three hundred years, filling a silence so old that half the circle flinched to hear it filled at all.

# 

# It was not, by any strict measure, a beautiful song. It was five different grammars of memory forced into one throat, clumsy in places, raw in others. But it was, for the first time in three hundred years, a telling that a stone-carver, an archivist, a trader, a forest-dweller, and a singer could each recognize a piece of in their own bones — and recognition, Wren was learning, mattered far more than beauty, when what you were trying to do was convince five strangers that they had once been kin.

# 

# When she finished, no one spoke for a long time.

# 

# It was Elder Korr, of all people, who broke the silence, and his voice when it came was rougher than Wren had ever heard an elder's voice, because elders were not, generally, permitted to sound uncertain.

# 

# "That's our word in it," he said. "Near the end. The old word for \*promise,\* the one that's carved nowhere in our halls because it isn't carved anywhere — it's only ever been sung. How does a Tideborn girl know a Deephewn word we don't even write down?"

# 

# "Because," Wren said, "I don't think it was ever a Deephewn word. I think it was everyone's word, before there was a Deephewn or a Tideborn to claim it separately. I think we were one people who borrowed more from the world than we could ever repay, and when the bill came due, we didn't have the courage to pay it together — so instead we tore ourselves into five, so that none of us would ever be tempted to spend what was left of the debt alone."

# 

# She looked around the circle — at Bryn, who already believed her; at Ash, whose stillness had the particular quality of someone who had suspected this all along and simply never had proof; at the Vantor archivist, going pale as the shape of every forged margin note in his own Archive suddenly made a different, worse kind of sense; at Corvan, already doing the terrible arithmetic of what an unpayable debt three hundred years old, split five ways, might actually cost.

# 

# "I don't know how to fix it," Wren said honestly. "I only know that none of us can, alone. Five separate people can be worn down to nothing, one at a time, by something patient enough to wait three hundred years. But I don't think it's ever once tried to reckon with all five of us standing in the same place, remembering the same thing, at the same time. I think that's the only arithmetic it hasn't already solved."

#

# 

# \## V. The Choice

# 

# The journey to the Blank took three days from the Crossing, and nobody spoke much on the road, which suited the mood better than conversation would have. Bryn walked with his charcoal rubbing wrapped in oilcloth and checked it so often Wren suspected he no longer trusted his own memory of what it said, as if the shaft itself might have changed the words while he wasn't looking. Master Feodor kept close to Corvan, of all people, the two of them trading the particular grim shorthand of men who each kept a different kind of book and had each, in their own way, spent years lying quietly in the margins of it. Ash walked alone, as she nearly always did, though twice Wren caught her glancing back at the rest of the group with an expression that might, on someone less practiced at letting things go, have been called hope.

# 

# They went to the Blank together, in the end, because there was nowhere else left to take the reckoning — the growing colourless wound at the edge of the Quiet Wood, larger now than it had been even a season ago, close enough that Ash said her people could feel its silence pressing on their teeth like weather before a storm.

# 

# Standing at its border was unlike anything Wren had imagined. It was not cold, exactly, or hot, or any temperature that made sense against skin. It was simply \*absent\* in a way that made the whole idea of temperature feel briefly beside the point, the way asking about the colour of an empty room makes you realize colour was never really the room's to begin with. Birds would not fly over it; Wren watched a starling bank hard away from the border a hundred yards out, as if it had struck an invisible wall, and she understood, with a lurch, that the starling had more sense about the place than five clans of grown people who had spent three centuries pretending it wasn't there at all.

# 

# They brought what they had. Bryn carried a rubbing of the First Stone's whole inscription, painstakingly copied in charcoal over three long nights. The Vantor archivist — his name, Wren finally learned, was Master Feodor, and he looked ten years older than he had a week before — brought the torn corner of the True Map with its five clustered settlements. Corvan brought the ledger itself, opened to its very first page, to an entry recorded in ink so faded it was nearly its own kind of blank, naming a debtor that was not a person and an amount that was not a number. Ash brought nothing at all, because the Empty Name was not a thing that could be carried in the hand; she had already given it away, and the giving away was the offering.

# 

# And Wren brought the Long Song, whole, as far whole as three hundred years of forgetting and one very determined fortnight of remembering could make it.

# 

# They stood at the Blank's edge and, one by one, laid down what they had carried — not into the absence itself, which took nothing that was merely offered at its border, but around it, in a rough circle, the way the Hollowkin laid down their yearly griefs.

# 

# Nothing happened. For a long, cold, terrible minute, nothing happened at all, and Wren felt something in her chest go hollow with the particular fear of having been wrong about everything, out loud, in front of five clans who had trusted her enough to follow her this far.

# 

# Then the Blank did not so much move as \*notice\* them — a pressure change, subtle as the moment before thunder, and every person standing at its edge felt, all at once, the unmistakable sensation of being weighed.

# 

# It did not ask a question in words. There were no words at its disposal, and perhaps had never been. But the shape of what it wanted settled into all five of them at the same moment, the way a debt makes itself known in a body before it makes itself known in a ledger:

# 

# \*the telling is not payment. The telling is only the account, finally written correctly. Something real must still be given, freely, or the account remains open, and I will go on collecting it, one boat, one meadow, one name, for as long as it takes.\*

# 

# The five delegations looked at one another, and for the first time in three hundred years, the argument that passed between them was not about whose fault the debt was, but about who would pay first.

# 

# It was Wren who moved first, because she understood — with the sudden, frightening clarity of a verse finally resolving after a lifetime of almost-understanding it — that a true payment could not be haggled over by committee. It had to be personal, or it would only ever be theft dressed up as sacrifice.

# 

# She thought of Uncle Fenn's boat, and the half-finished knot in Aunt Mira's net, and the particular ache of being the only person left in the world who remembered exactly how he laughed. She had carried that memory like a coal for weeks, afraid to set it down, afraid that setting it down meant losing him twice.

# 

# She set it down anyway — not the fact of him, not his name, but the private, unshared, aching \*shape\* of missing him that only she still carried, the last resistant knot of grief that had refused, until this moment, to be shared with anyone. She let it go into the space at the border of the Blank, the way the Hollowkin let go of their griefs each year, in love rather than force.

# 

# It did not disappear. It simply stopped being only hers. She felt it, oddly, settle instead into the song itself, into the Long Song, where it would be sung, imperfectly, by other voices, for other reasons, long after she herself had stopped carrying it alone.

# 

# One by one, the others understood, and followed.

# 

# Bryn asked the Deephewn elders, that same night by messenger-bird, for permission to finally, formally, and forever seal the deepest shaft — not out of fear this time, but \*(manuset fortsetter ikke lenger enn dette ennå)\*
